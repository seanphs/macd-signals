What this page is. Once a night, a free automated job downloads a year of daily closing prices for about 800 stocks and funds, runs a momentum indicator called MACD over them, and notes the days when momentum looked like it was topping out (a PEAK) or bottoming out (a VALLEY). This page shows those results for the tickers on your own list, for most trading days of the past year. It describes what the indicators are doing. It does not tell you what to buy or sell, and no indicator can predict prices.

The basics

Ticker / symbol
The short code a stock or fund trades under, like AAPL for Apple or VOO for a Vanguard S&P 500 fund. BRK-B is Berkshire Hathaway class B shares.
Close
The last price of the trading day. Everything here is computed from daily closes only.
Trading day / bar
One day the market was open. "Bars" is chart jargon for one day's data. Weekends and holidays are skipped, so 5 bars is one week and about 21 bars is one month.
As of
The job runs at night after the U.S. market closes, so the newest day on the page is the last completed trading day. Today's partial trading is never included. The day picker in the header lets you look at any earlier trading day of the past year once the indicator has enough history: MACD needs about 33 trading days of prices before its first value, so the picker starts there (roughly seven weeks after the oldest price on the page).
Holdings
Tickers you own. You enter them on the My list tab. They always appear in the All holdings table, with or without a signal.
Watchlist
Tickers you do not own but keep an eye on. In the Watchlist table they appear only on days they fire a signal, unless you tick "show all"; they are always available in the Signal timeline and on the Charts tab.
ETF / fund
A basket of many stocks or bonds traded as one ticker, such as VOO or QQQ. Funds that hold short-term U.S. Treasury bills (SGOV, BIL and similar) barely move, so MACD says little about them.

Moving averages and MACD

Moving average
The average close over the last N days, recomputed each day. It smooths out daily noise so you can see the direction of travel.
EMA
Exponential moving average: a moving average that gives more weight to recent days, so it reacts faster than a plain average.
MACD
Moving Average Convergence Divergence. The MACD line is the 12-day EMA minus the 26-day EMA. When it is above zero, recent prices are running above the longer-term trend (momentum is up); below zero, momentum is down. It is quoted in price units, so a MACD of +3.9 on a $344 stock is small, and the page also shows it as a percent of price.
Signal line
A 9-day EMA of the MACD line, so a smoothed, lagging copy of it. The three numbers "12/26/9" are these settings and are the standard ones.
Histogram
MACD line minus signal line, drawn as bars. Green bars above zero mean MACD is above its signal line; red bars below zero mean it is below. Bars that are shrinking toward zero mean the two lines are converging, which is momentum fading. Growing bars mean momentum is building.
Crossover
The moment the MACD line crosses the signal line. The histogram flips sign and changes colour. Crossing downward is a bearish crossover (peak); crossing upward is a bullish crossover (valley).
Zero line
MACD at zero means the 12-day and 26-day averages are equal. Peak signals that happen above zero and valley signals below zero are considered more meaningful than ones near zero.

The signals

Each signal shows only on the day it first appears. A ticker can fire two stages on the same day if things move fast. The three stages, from earliest and least reliable to latest and most reliable:

1. Early warning
The histogram has shrunk two days in a row after reaching a high (peak) or low (valley). Momentum is fading, but nothing has crossed yet. Many early warnings fizzle out.
2. MACD turned
The MACD line itself changed direction: turned down while above zero (peak) or turned up while below zero (valley).
3. CONFIRMED crossover
The MACD line crossed its signal line. This is the classic MACD signal that most traders watch. It is the most reliable of the three but also the latest, often days after the actual price turn.
PEAK
Momentum may be topping out. The price may still rise, but the rate of climb is slowing or reversing. Peaks on holdings are at the top of the dashboard because they are the ones most people want to notice first.
VALLEY
Momentum may be bottoming out. The decline is slowing or reversing.
Choppy
MACD is hugging zero (under 0.25% of the price). In that zone the lines cross back and forth constantly and the signals mean little. Normal for T-bill and Treasury funds like SGOV and VGIT.
P1, P2, P3, V1, V2, V3
Short codes used in the timeline grid: P for peak, V for valley, number for the stage.

The other numbers

RSI (14)
Relative Strength Index over 14 days, a 0 to 100 score of how strong recent gains have been compared with recent losses. Above 70 is conventionally called "overbought" (a strong run that may be stretched), below 30 "oversold". Around 50 is neutral. The meters mark 30 and 70 and turn red at 70 or above and blue at 30 or below.
Below 1-yr high
How far the close is below the highest close of the year of history on this page, up to the selected day, in percent. 0% means it closed at a new one-year high. A peak signal within a couple of percent of the high is flagged "near 1-yr high".
Near highs, no turn yet
Holdings within 2% of their one-year high with RSI of 65 or more that have not fired any signal that day. Strong, but no sign of turning. Choppy tickers are left out: a money-market fund is always at its high, which says nothing.
MACD vs signal
"above, falling" means the MACD line is above the signal line (histogram green) but the gap is closing; "below, rising" means below (red) with the gap closing; and so on.
Histogram (sparkline)
The last N histogram bars in miniature, N set by the range selector. Green above the line, red below.
Recent
A strip of the trading days in the selected range, oldest on the left. A coloured mark is a day that ticker fired a signal; hover for details.
Stage tracker
The three boxes on each card. Filled boxes are stages seen in the last 10 trading days; the outlined box is the selected day's.
Data from <date>
A small badge that appears when a ticker's newest data is older than the selected day, for example when last night's download for it failed and the previous night's file was kept.
Screener
The Screener tab lists every ticker in the data set (about 800) that fired a signal on the last completed trading day (or, under Recovering, crossed up within the last few days), not just the ones on your list, so you can look for valleys and recoveries on stocks you do not own yet. It covers the latest day only; the day picker does not apply to it. The filters you set are remembered in this browser. "Add to watch" puts a ticker on your watch list; clicking a ticker opens its chart even if it is not on your list.
Recovering
A valley crossover (stage 3) within the last N trading days where the histogram is still positive and the MACD line is still rising: momentum turned up recently and has kept going so far. A recovery can fail; the next day can turn the histogram negative again.
Last crossover
The most recent confirmed crossover (stage 3) in either direction and how many trading days ago it happened; 0 means it was on the latest day.
1M / 3M change
The close compared with the close 21 / 63 trading days earlier (about one and three months), in percent.
Group
Where the ticker sits in the site's list: Indexes, S&P 500, Nasdaq-100, ETFs, Other stocks or Added by the owner. A company in both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq-100 is listed once, under S&P 500.
Holding details
On the My list tab you can enter, for each holding, how many shares you own, your average cost per share, the date you bought, a sell target and a trailing stop percentage. Every box is optional. The details are kept in this browser only: they are never sent anywhere and the shareable link never carries them. The Backup box on that tab is how you move them to another device.
Value / Weight / Gain since cost
Value is shares times the close on the selected day; Weight is that value as a share of the total value of your holdings with shares; Gain since cost is how far the close is from your average cost, in percent (and in money when shares are entered); a negative number is a loss. The Day change stat is the change in value from the previous trading day's close.
Sell target
A price you chose. The page shows how far the close is from it ("4.2% below", "2.1% above") and lists holdings that closed at or above it or within 3% below it under "Your targets and stops". The number is yours; the page does not suggest one.
Trailing stop
A percentage you chose. The stop price is the highest close since your purchase date (or over the past year of data when no date is entered, or the date is before the data starts) minus that percentage; it moves up with new highs and never down. The page shows how far the close is above it, or BELOW when the close is under it. It only measures; it does not place orders or suggest a percentage.
Held / long-term
How long you have held the shares, from your purchase date to the selected day. "Long-term" marks a holding kept more than one year (a year and a day or more). Tax rules depend on your own situation and country; the page does not give tax advice.
vs SPY (1M)
This ticker's change over the last 21 trading days minus SPY's change over the same days, in percentage points. SPY is a fund that tracks the S&P 500, so this is "did it do better or worse than the broad U.S. market this month". It shows a dash when SPY's data is not available.
Track record
On the Charts tab, under the chart: for the ticker shown, what the close did 5, 10 and 20 trading days after each signal in the year of history on this page, up to the selected day. Each kind of signal (peak or valley, each stage) gets a hit rate and a median move, next to the base rate: what the close did after any trading day, signal or not. The samples are small: a year rarely holds more than about ten signals of one kind, so most of these numbers rest on a handful of cases, and the page mutes any that rest on fewer than five. "Show each signal" lists every signal with its date and moves. It describes what happened on this one ticker in the past; it says nothing about what the next signal will do.
Hit rate
The share of past signals that were followed by a close in the direction the signal names: for a peak, a lower close 5, 10 or 20 trading days later; for a valley, a higher close. An unchanged close is a miss either way. Shown as "4 of 6": 4 hits out of the 6 signals old enough to have an outcome; hover for the percent and the base rate. A signal whose horizon runs past the selected day has no outcome yet and is left out of the count.
Base rate
How often the close was lower (or higher) that many trading days after any day at all, signal or not, over the same history. It is what gives a hit rate its meaning: a peak hit rate of 55% next to an "Any day, lower" rate of 52% means the signal added almost nothing on that ticker; a hit rate well above the base rate means it did better than an ordinary day did.
Median move
The middle value of the percent change in the close over the horizon, across the signals in the group: half the moves were larger, half smaller. Signed, so -2.3% means the typical close was 2.3% lower. A median is less swayed by one extreme case than an average would be.
Peak record (10d)
A column of the All holdings table: the hit rate of that holding's confirmed PEAK signals over the next 10 trading days, up to the selected day, as "4 of 6" with the percent and the base rate in the hover text. A dash means no confirmed peak so far in its history, or none old enough to have a 10-day outcome yet (the hover text says which); muted numbers rest on fewer than five outcomes. The full record, for every kind of signal and every horizon, is on the Charts tab.

Reading the charts

The top panel is the closing price. The dashed line is the one-year high up to the selected day. The middle panel is the MACD line (blue), the signal line (orange) and the histogram (green and red bars) with a dotted zero line. Markers on the MACD line are days a signal fired, coloured by stage. The bottom panel is RSI, with dotted lines at 30 and 70. Hover anywhere to read the exact values for that day.

A typical peak sequence on the chart: the green histogram bars get shorter (early warning), the blue MACD line flattens and tips over (MACD turned), then the blue line drops through the orange line and the bars turn red (CONFIRMED).

Things to keep in mind

MACD is built from averages, so it lags. By the time a crossover is confirmed, part of the move has already happened. It also gives plenty of false alarms in sideways markets. Treat these signals as a prompt to look, not as a verdict. A valley signal on a stock in a long decline is common on the way down; the "% below 1-yr high" column shows how far it has fallen, not whether it has stopped. The distance to a sell target or a trailing stop is arithmetic on a number you typed in, not a forecast of where the price is going. A track-record hit rate close to the base rate means the signal added nothing on that ticker over the past year, and even one well above it is a handful of cases from a single year. Prices come from Yahoo Finance and can occasionally be late or missing; the footer shows how many tickers failed or were kept from the previous night. Nothing here is financial advice.
